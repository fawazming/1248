A4. Literature Review & Scientific Review [Lecture 4]

Definition: A literature review is a systematic, comprehensive and critical method for identifying, evaluating and interpreting the work produced by researchers, scholars and practitioners. It is an interpretation and synthesis of published work - "telling the story" of your research area.

What a literature review should do

  • Compare & contrast authors' views; group authors with similar conclusions.
  • Criticise aspects of methodology; note areas of disagreement.
  • Highlight exemplary studies and gaps in research.
  • Show how your study relates to previous studies and to the literature generally; conclude with a summary.

Why write a literature review

  • To define and limit the problem; place the study in historical perspective.
  • To avoid unnecessary duplication; evaluate promising research methods.
  • To relate findings to previous knowledge and suggest further research.

Critical analysis - evaluating literature (CRVEC-L)

CriterionQuestion to ask
RelevancyIs it directly related to your topic?
ObjectivityIs there bias/agenda that might skew the information?
ReliabilityIs it trustworthy? (journals are generally reliable, but still question them)
AccuracyDoes it fit what you know? Any contradictions? Are references cited?
CurrencyIs it up to date? (except historical events/theory - supplement with modern analysis)
LegitimacyDoes it support/refute your topic, or highlight recommendations your topic addresses?

Sources: Theses/dissertations - journal articles - conference proceedings - online publications. Databases: Scopus, ISI Web of Knowledge, IEEE, ACM, Springer, PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE; grey literature (reports, white papers, pre/post-prints).

Search Strategy: Break the question into concepts; gather synonyms/abbreviations/variants; define inclusion & exclusion criteria; use Boolean logic; limit by subject, language, year; iterate.

BooleanANDORNOT
EffectNarrows - both termsBroadens - either termExcludes a term

Structure & do's / don'ts

Structure: Introduction (define topic, justify, scope) -> Main body (organised by themes, general->specific) -> Conclusion (summarise, evaluate, identify gaps).

DO: review, summarise, synthesise, compare/contrast, organise. DON'T: report, patch, copy-&-paste, repeat, be disoriented.
Plagiarism = failure/blacklisting.

Problem Statement: A brief description of the issues that need to be addressed (1-2 sentences): what is the problem (negative situation)? why does it matter? whose perspective? Use the FINER criteria - Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant.

A5. Qualitative Research [Lecture 5-1]

Definition: Qualitative research is "non-numeric", interpretive research using rich verbal descriptions (interviews, documents, participant observation) to understand and explain social phenomena - in their natural setting and in context.

Objectivist (Quantitative)Subjectivist (Qualitative)
Everyone agrees on what is good/rightNo single truth when people are involved
Numerical assessment: experimentsVerbal descriptions
Pre-selected subjects, variables, methodsExpert judgment of emerging themes
Controlled environmentNatural environment
Answers What? (performance, behavioural change)Answers Why? and How?

Essential characteristics

  • Concerned with understanding a phenomenon; assumes multiple realities.
  • Data are rich verbal descriptions; researcher is immersed and in direct contact (researcher = instrument).
  • Highly interactive; methodology is flexible/emergent; holistic and context-sensitive.
  • Constructs meaning from the participant's point of view (informants, not subjects).
  • Explores open questions rather than testing hypotheses; employs purposive sampling.

Data collection techniques ("fieldwork")

Observation - describe the setting; first-hand experience; observer as outsider (unobtrusive) vs participant observation. Beware the Hawthorne effect (subjects change behaviour because they know they are studied). Roles: complete observer -> observer-as-participant -> participant-as-observer -> complete participant.

Interviewing - one-on-one; elicits feelings, thoughts, opinions, experiences. Types: informal conversational; general guide; standardised open-ended (unstructured); closed fixed-response (structured); semi-structured. Strategies: funnelling, story-telling, probing, linking, puzzled, encouraging.

Focus Groups - group discussion of a topic.

Document Analysis - texts, sound, photos, videos; negotiate access; respect confidentiality. Varieties: conversational, discourse, narrative, objective hermeneutic.

Sampling:Purposive sampling - select people/sites who can best help understand the phenomenon. Sample size determined by saturation - the point where no new cases and redundant information keep coming up.

Quality:Triangulation (multiple data sources, subjects, methods, kinds of data) - Member checks - Construct validity - Reliability with an audit trail.

Qualitative data-analysis process

Reading/ Immersion -> Coding -> Displaying Data -> Questioning/ Verification -> Data Reduction -> Interpretation

Classic 3 steps: Data Reduction -> Data Display -> Conclusion Drawing & Verification. Coding = tags that categorise data to assign meaning (a priori codes = expected; grounded codes = discovered). Tools: NVivo, Atlas.ti, Ethnograph.

Common qualitative research types

TypeKey idea
Case StudyStudy a phenomenon in its real context when phenomenon/context boundaries are unclear. Types: intrinsic, instrumental, multiple.
EthnographyRooted in anthropology (ethno=people, graphy=describing); researcher immerses in the community; also called participant observation/naturalistic enquiry.
Grounded TheoryGlaser & Strauss; theory generated from data (constant comparative analysis); theory must be faithful to the evidence.
Action ResearchRepeated cycle: Planning -> Action -> Reflection; aim is effective action, improved practice, implementing change.
Design-based ResearchKnowledge built while designing, building & evaluating an artifact. Cycle: Awareness -> Suggestion -> Development -> Evaluation -> Conclusion.

A6. Quantitative Data Analysis [Lecture 5-2]

Definition:Statistics is the science and practice of developing human knowledge through the use of empirical data expressed in quantitative form; based on statistical theory (a branch of applied mathematics).
Purpose of analysis: describe/summarise data; search for patterns/themes; answer your research questions.

Descriptive statistics - describe a variable (mean, median, mode, frequencies, SD).

Inferential statistics - make inferences about a population from a random sample.

Variables & levels of measurement

Independent variable (IV) - believed to influence the outcome. Dependent variable (DV) - influenced by the IV / the variable you predict.

LevelPropertyExamples
NominalCategories, no orderRace, gender, eye colour
OrdinalOrdered; unequal intervalsLow/middle/high income, Likert scale, rankings
IntervalEqual distance; arbitrary zeroTemperature ( degF)
RatioEqual distance + true zeroWeight, height, age, salary

Descriptive statistics

Mean - sum divided by number; affected by outliers; interval/ratio.

Median - middle value; robust to outliers/skew; ordinal/interval/ratio.

Mode - most frequent; nominal/ordinal.

Range - max min; simple but outlier-sensitive.

Variance - average squared deviation from the mean.

Std deviation - spread in same units as data.

Skewness - degree of asymmetry (0 = symmetric; near 0 is desirable for normal data). Kurtosis - peakedness (normal = 0; platykurtic <0 flat, leptokurtic >0 peaked). Values between 2 and +2 are acceptable for normal univariate distribution (George & Mallery, 2010).

Inferential statistics & hypothesis testing

  • Population - group the researcher wishes to study; Sample - subset of the population; Census - data from all units (no sampling).
  • H0 (null) vs H1 (alternative). Goal: can we reject the null hypothesis?
  • Alpha (alpha), the significance level, is pre-determined by the researcher (e.g. .05).
  • Statistically significant = probability of the finding by chance alone is less than 5%.
Decision (from sample)Null Hypothesis TRUENull Hypothesis FALSE
Reject NullType I Error (alpha)Correct decision
Fail to reject NullCorrect decisionType II Error (beta)

Type I = rejecting a true null. Type II = failing to reject a false null.

Parametric tests - assume data from a normal distribution; require interval/ratio data (e.g. t-test, ANOVA, Pearson, regression).

Non-parametric tests - no normality assumption; used with nominal/ordinal data (e.g. Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Chi-square, Spearman).

Normality tests: Shapiro-Wilk (most powerful) - Anderson-Darling - Lilliefors - Kolmogorov-Smirnov.
Analyses: Univariate (1 variable) - Bivariate (2 variables) - Multivariate (>2 variables).
