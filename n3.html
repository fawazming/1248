A7. Mixed Methods Research [Lecture 6]

Definition: In mixed methods, the researcher collects and analyses both qualitative and quantitative data, integrates/links them (concurrently or sequentially or embedded), gives priority to one or both, and frames the procedures within philosophical worldviews and specific research designs.

When to use / why / challenges

When: one data source is insufficient - need to explain initial results - generalise exploratory findings - enhance a study with a second method - employ a theoretical stance - understand an objective across multiple phases.

Advantages: strengths of one method complement the weaknesses of the other - more evidence - flexible use of tools - answers questions one type cannot - multiple worldviews - practical (numbers + words).

Challenges: do we have the skills? - time and resources (expensive; qualitative is slower) - can we convince others (relatively new)?

Key design decisions

  • Interaction: Independent (mix only at final interpretation) vs Interactive (mix before).
  • Priority: Equal - Quantitative priority - Qualitative priority.
  • Timing: Concurrent (single phase) - Sequential (two phases) - Multiphase.
  • Where/how to mix: during interpretation - during data analysis - during data collection.

The four major mixed-methods designs

DesignTimingPriorityPurpose / how it works
Convergent ParallelConcurrentEqualCollect both forms at the same time, analyse separately, then merge/compare at interpretation.
EmbeddedConcurrent or SequentialOne primaryOne study is nested within the other (before/during/after the major component).
Explanatory SequentialSequential (Quant -> Qual)QuantitativeQuantitative results are explained/followed up by a qualitative phase.
Exploratory SequentialSequential (Qual -> Quant)QualitativeQualitative findings build to a quantitative phase (e.g. instrument development).

For your thesis: For a Master's, "Q only" is appropriate; mixed methods is more typical of a PhD. Your proposed DSR study (quantitative evaluation + clinician-facing qualitative usability checks) is closest to an embedded / exploratory sequential design.

A8. Formal Methods & Research Methodology [Lecture 7]

Definition:Formal means using logic (or mathematics) to describe the model/algorithm, and evaluating the model using a mathematical approach - e.g. theorem proving, model checking, algorithm complexity analysis.

Modelling / Specification -> Refinement -> Implementation

How to represent a formal model

  • Formal specification languages: CSP, Z, VDM, B-method, RAISE.
  • Formal languages: context-free grammar, regular grammar.
  • Finite state machines (automata theory, graph theory).
  • You may propose a new one.

How to evaluate a model

Non-formal model: experiment (standard dataset) - usability testing - simulation.

Formal model: model checking - theorem proving - algorithm analysis (Big-O).

Model checking: input = model + properties; output = yes/no, and if no, a counter-example. Theorem proving: input = model + properties (theorem); output = proof/hypothesis & conclusion.

Relevance in CS research: Formal methods give rigour, precision and unambiguity; enable validation and verification; support proof of properties such as accuracy, security, trustworthiness, correctness, reliability and consistency (e.g. e-voting, authentication protocols, ATM); and allow early error detection before costly implementation.

A9. Thesis Writing & Scientific Writing [Lecture 8]

Definition: A thesis is "a written work resulting from original research, especially one submitted for a higher degree in a university." It must demonstrate: an understanding of the state of the art, a critical appreciation of existing work, a novel contribution, and systematic evaluation.

Parts of a thesis

Initial pages: Title page - Declaration - Acknowledgements - Abstract (& translation) - Contents - List of Tables - List of Illustrations - List of Symbols/Abbreviations.

Chapter titles: 1. Introduction - 2. Literature Review - 3. Research Methodology / Material & Method - 4. Experiment, Result & Analysis - 5. Conclusions & Recommendations.

Abstract: A brief summary of the entire thesis; contains no new information; no longer than 1 page A4; 150 words (MSc) / 350 words (PhD); write it last. Covers: problem (why), purpose (what), methods (how), results (findings), conclusion (knowledge learned).
Common mistakes: too much background/methods, references, abbreviations.

Key section content

SectionContent
IntroductionGeneral background -> narrower context -> rationale/problem statement -> connecting problem to objective (hypothesis at the end). What we know / gap / what the study shows.
Statement of ProblemMust be reflected in the title; not answerable by yes/no; arranged in the flow of the study.
Scope & DelimitationScope = coverage of the study; Delimitation/Limitation = limitations of the study/topic.
MethodologyEnough detail to replicate; research design, population & samples, instrument, validation (validity/reliability), procedure, data gathering, data processing & statistical treatment, ethics.
Results & DiscussionAll data supporting/refuting the hypothesis; tables for precise values, figures for trends; describe trends/significance; discuss meaning and link to literature.
Conclusions & RecommendationsGeneralised statements answering the problems; may be micro->macro; recommendations based on findings, feasible/doable; future work.

Scientific-writing quick rules

  • Optimal sentence length ~ 15-20 words; average paragraph ~ 150 words; a paragraph should have a topic sentence + content + conclusion.
  • Prefer active voice (journals prefer it); passive is common in Methods/Results.
  • Numbers: use numerals for 10 and above, words below 10; write out numbers beginning a sentence.
  • Cite all evidence (else = plagiarism); follow the required reference style (UKM style).
